I figured out I was trans at a pretty early age (13) but for a while I identified as non-binary because I couldn't understand why I still wanted to have longish hair, painted nails and wear eyeliner if I wanted to "look trans" (I was convinced that in order to be taken seriously as a trans man, I had to look super masculine). Last year, right before I was given the green light to start using testosterone, I went to the concert in Stockholm (the concert was on June 14th and my gender clinic appointment was on June 17th). I wasn't a "dedicated fan" of MCR before seeing them live, I had listened to some songs off Revenge and TBP, and that was all (I was a massive fan of Frank's solo music which is the reason I went in the first place). But seeing Frank, Ray and Gerard with long hair and them being confident about it, I had the revelation that maybe I don't have to have super short hair to be taken seriously as a man. So the night before I went to my gender clinic appointment, I had a very emotional talk with my mum (my biggest supporter) about starting using testosterone. I wasn't 100% convinced it would be the right decision for me (which it was, it fixed my brain chemistry and has helped me with my depression), but I explained to my mum that I wanted to look like "that" (pulling out pictures of the band with long-ish hair and makeup from Revenge era and after that) without having severe gender dysphoria, which helped her understand me and what i wanted out of my transition a bit more. I finally know I don't have to have short hair and never use makeup to be a man. I can paint my nails and that doesn't make me any less of a man. I also nearly cried after seeing Gerard in the cheerleading dress because for me it was the epitome of trans joy. Seeing someone as famous as them have an actual good time wearing a dress and being confident about it was beautiful to see, and extremely important to me. Trans joy and seeing others experience trans joy is more important to me than anything else. There aren't a lot of public figures that openly and strongly support the trans community, and having a band I like have look up to and feel supported by in that sense is something I'm so thankful for. When “I’m Not Okay” came out, I was a sophomore in high school. I grew up in West Orange, New Jersey, which is two towns over from where Gerard and Mikey are from, and from where the band as a whole is from. There was something relatable to me about the opening scene of the video for I’m Not Okay - the front door stoop. Before MCR entered my world, my emerging preteen tomboyish vibe was working class masculine practicality. I was fresh out of catholic school and I left its bullies and uniforms behind forever. So I explored gender with newfound fashion freedom and clothes from the boys section from stores at the Livingston Mall. But when MCR came on MTV, I saw myself in something I didn’t have words for yet. No one was using the word “genderqueer.” The gender the band presented showed me that masculinity could be glam, could be gorgeous, and it could also be punk. It felt amazing and beautiful. And their confidence felt impossible : the way they stood or moved around while singing or playing, the unapologetic taking up space. Because so often my own body language at the time was about trying to make myself small, to disappear as much as I could - either because of the abuse I faced at home, or because of the physical debilitating pain and dysphoria from my periods. Twenty years later, I’m in a new era of exploring my gender even more now, and I’m trying to bring back the confidence I had when I was younger. When I sing along to MCR, I try to sing from my chest voice, where my range is lower than how I’m normally used to hearing it. I still rewatch the old MCR videos, that I saw on MTV decades ago - and now I’m taking notes on body language and posture. The confidence they have doesn’t seem so impossible to me anymore. -Resi Ibañez
